1 · Overview

Brownian motion — the incessant, jagged movement of microscopic bodies suspended in a fluid — was the first direct experimental window onto the atomic nature of matter and the statistical mechanics of Boltzmann, as formalized by Einstein in 1905. In a fluid at thermal equilibrium, the fluctuations of a suspended particle's velocity reflect thermal fluctuations and obey equilibrium statistical mechanics. This experiment asks what happens when the bath itself is alive: a suspension of swimming bacteria that continuously convert stored chemical energy into mechanical motion, driving the system far from equilibrium.

The strategy, following Wu and Libchaber [9], is to watch passive micron-scale beads immersed in the bacterial bath. Their motion reports on the non-equilibrium dynamics of the medium: how much the fluctuations exceed thermal levels, whether the displacement statistics remain diffusive, and how the answer depends on bacterial concentration and activity. A second, independent module uses optical tweezers to measure forces on a trapped bead directly — calibrating the trap via confined Brownian motion and measuring the fluid viscosity via Stokes drag — which together with a diffusion measurement provides a test of the Einstein relation.

Why this is interesting. An active bath violates the assumptions behind the fluctuation–dissipation theorem. The beads can display "superdiffusive" motion at short times, an effective temperature two to three orders of magnitude above room temperature, and a breakdown of energy equipartition — all measurable with a microscope, a camera, and careful particle tracking.

2 · Theory: equilibrium Brownian motion

2.1 The Langevin equation

A microscopic object in a fluid experiences a drag force and a rapidly fluctuating random force from molecular collisions. In the overdamped limit relevant here (inertia negligible on observation timescales), the Langevin equation reads

F = −γ ẋ + η(t) = 0,   ⟨η(t)⟩ = 0,   ⟨ηi(t) ηj(t′)⟩ = 2γD δij δ(t − t′)

where γ is the friction coefficient, η a white-noise random force, ⟨·⟩ an average over realizations, and i, j spatial indices. Solving gives vanishing mean displacement and the hallmark of diffusion,

⟨x(t)⟩ = 0,    ⟨x2(t)⟩ = 2dDt

with d the number of dimensions and D the diffusion coefficient. The linear growth of the mean-squared displacement (MSD) with time is the signature of diffusive behavior; any departure, ⟨x2(t)⟩ ∝ tα with α ≠ 1, signals anomalous transport.

2.2 The Einstein relation

In thermal equilibrium the random force originates solely from thermal motion of the fluid molecules, and the fluctuation–dissipation theorem ties the diffusion coefficient to temperature and to the particle's mobility μ (velocity per unit drag force, μ = v/Fdrag):

D = μ kBT   (Einstein, 1905)

For a sphere of radius R, Stokes' law gives μ = 1/(6πηR) with η the dynamic viscosity; non-spherical objects keep the same size and viscosity scaling with a different numerical prefactor. The standard derivation proceeds from the fluctuation–dissipation theorem — see §2 of Kubo's classic review [1].

In the presence of active (non-thermal, energy-consuming) fluctuations the Einstein relation need not hold: D can depend on the strength and form of the active forcing, and on the shape and size of the tracers. One may nonetheless define an effective temperature by forcing the relation, Teff ≡ D/(μkB) — a diagnostic, not a thermodynamic temperature.

Refresher — mobility and Stokes drag

A sphere dragged slowly through a viscous fluid feels Fdrag = 6πηRv (creeping flow, Re ≪ 1). The mobility μ = 1/(6πηR) is the terminal velocity per unit force. Everything in this experiment is overdamped: inertial relaxation times (m/γ ~ 10−5 s for a 10 μm bead) are far below the 30 ms frame time.

3 · Active media and the bacterial bath

Bacillus subtilis are non-pathogenic, rod-shaped bacteria that swim by rotating bundles of flagella. Their motion is a run-and-tumble walk: straight "runs" of order a second punctuated by abrupt "tumbles" that randomize direction — the canonical model of a self-propelled particle [2]. At high concentration they can switch to collective swarming motion.

Because each bacterium continuously injects mechanical energy at the scale of its own body (~1–2 μm), a bacterial suspension is a fluid stirred from within at small scales. The central questions for a passive tracer immersed in it are: How are the injected energy and momentum distributed? Do tracer statistics remain diffusive? Is there anything left of equilibrium relations like D = μkBT or equipartition?

Refresher — run-and-tumble, in one paragraph

A self-propelled particle with speed v0 whose direction decorrelates over a persistence time τ has, in equilibrium-like fashion, an effective long-time diffusivity D ~ v02τ/d. At times t ≪ τ the motion is ballistic (MSD ∝ t2); at t ≫ τ it is diffusive (MSD ∝ t). The crossover time and length are set by the persistence — in the bacterial bath, by the lifetime of collective structures rather than of individual runs.

4 · The Wu–Libchaber experiment (Phys. Rev. Lett. 84, 3017, 2000)

The lab's bacterial-bath module follows the landmark measurement of Wu and Libchaber [9]: micron-scale polystyrene beads in a bath of swimming E. coli, confined to a freely suspended horizontal soap film. The film makes the geometry quasi-two-dimensional (its thickness ≈ the bead diameter) and slashes hydrodynamic dissipation to the surroundings, since the shear viscosity of air is only ~1% of the film's. Wildtype E. coli RP437 (body ~1 × 2–3 μm, flagella rotating at ~100 Hz) were transformed to express GFP for fluorescence velocimetry; stock concentration n ≈ 5.4×1010 cells/cm3, diluted as needed, with the nonionic surfactant IGEPAL held at its critical micellar concentration (verified not to affect swimming).

4.1 Two regimes in the MSD

Beads as large as 10 μm — for which thermal Brownian motion is undetectable on these scales — fluctuated wildly in the bacterial bath. Their MSD, computed from ensembles of 10–100 tracks up to 5 minutes long, displayed a clean crossover:

  • Short times (0.03 s < t ≲ 1 s): superdiffusive, ⟨Δr2(t)⟩ ~ tα with 1.5 < α < 2.0. Tracks consist of straight segments — ballistic-like motion.
  • Long times (10 s < t < 300 s): normal diffusion recovered, α ≈ 1.0, as the tracks randomize.

The crossover is not the underdamped–overdamped transition of ordinary Brownian motion: the inertial damping time of a 10 μm bead is ~10−5 s, hopelessly short. Instead it reflects the transient formation of coherent structures — swirls and jets of typical scale 10–20 μm — in the bacterial bath. Their lifetime tC sets the crossover, and tC grows linearly with bacterial density n.

4.2 A Langevin model with correlated noise

The minimal model that captures both regimes replaces the white noise with an exponentially correlated "collisional force":

m dv/dt = −γv + f(t),   ⟨f(0)·f(t)⟩ = (4Dγ2/tC) e−t/tC

which yields a velocity autocorrelation ⟨v(t)·v(0)⟩ = ⟨v(0)2⟩ e−t/tC with ⟨v(0)2⟩ = 2D/tC, and hence the closed form

⟨Δr2(t)⟩ = 4Dt [1 − e−t/tC]   (Wu–Libchaber Eq. 5)

with asymptotes (D/tC)t2 for t ≪ tC and Dt for t ≫ tC. Plotting ⟨r(t)2⟩/4DtC against t/tC for all concentrations collapses the data onto the universal curve f(x) = x(1 − e−x).

4.3 Concentration scaling and the failure of equipartition

Fitting Eq. (5) to the data at densities n = 0.67, 1.34, 5.35 × 1010 cm−3 gives, to good approximation, linear growth of all three characteristic quantities with n: the long-time diffusivity D, the crossover time tC, and the coherent length ℓC ≡ √⟨Δr2(tC)⟩ (reaching tC ≈ 2 s and ℓC ≈ 10 μm — the visual swirl size — at the highest n). Meanwhile the beads' rms velocity is independent of n: vrms ≈ 5.0 ± 1.5 μm/s, consistent with the scaling check ℓC ≈ vrmstC.

Two beads of different size (4.5 and 10 μm) at the same n gave D ∝ 1/R — the same radius dependence as Stokes–Einstein — but with an effective temperature, defined through Teff = D·6πηR/kB, of 2–3 orders of magnitude above room temperature. Writing vrms2/v02 = (a/R)b, equilibrium equipartition would force b = 3 (energy shared equally per degree of freedom); the data instead suggest b = 1. The authors conclude that "the simple mechanism of equipartition of energy does not apply to our system and that hydrodynamic interactions must play a significant role" [9].

What to carry into the lab. The paper's logic is the template for your analysis: measure tracks → MSD → identify regimes on a log-log plot → fit Eq. (5) → extract D, tC, α as functions of bacterial concentration/activity → ask whether anything equilibrium-like survives.

4.4 The bacteria themselves are Maxwellian

A striking detail: the swimming-speed distribution of the bacteria in the film obeyed a Maxwell distribution, P(v) = (2|v|/v02) exp(−v2/v02) with v0 ≈ 20 μm/s — at high densities, collisions and hydrodynamic dispersion randomize swimming directions "identical to that of an ideal gas" [9], even though the bath is violently out of equilibrium energetically.

5 · This experiment: protocol

5.1 Samples

  • Beads in water (equilibrium control), beads in the bacteria suspension (the active bath), beads in bacterial supernatant ("sup") (controls for chemistry without swimmers), and optionally soap water.
  • Decide the bead-suspension-to-bath ratio; mix with pipettes; place a drop on the sample holder and stretch the droplet.
  • Bacteria: B. subtilis cultured the day before (seed from −80 °C stock into 3–4 ml LB, incubate ~16 h at 30 °C shaking ≥ 200 RPM, unsealed; incubate a sterile LB tube as a contamination control). Morning of the experiment: dilute ~30 μl into warm sterile LB, incubate several hours until slightly cloudy. Concentrate by centrifuge (10 min at 5000 g is standard and safe for the cells) to raise concentration, collect sup, or resuspend in PBS (no nutrients → arrests multiplication).

5.2 Microscopy and recording

  • Beads — fluorescence, movies of ~30–60 s.
  • Bacteria — phase contrast or brightfield, a few seconds of video or stills, taken before and after each fluorescence movie (documents activity over the session).
  • Let each movie finish writing to disk before starting the next capture — interrupting a write can destroy both files.

5.3 From movies to tracks

  • Bandpass filter each frame to suppress pixel noise and large-scale intensity gradients — choose the parameters and inspect the result visually.
  • Feature detection: extract bright spots; compute subpixel positions as the intensity-weighted centroid ("center of mass") of each bead's signal [15–17].
  • Linking: connect detections frame-to-frame into trajectories — again, choose parameters and verify on a rendered overlay.

5.4 From tracks to physics (MSD analysis)

  • Analyze the ensemble with the course-modified MSDAnalyzer MATLAB package (see §9).
  • Apply drift correction; keep only movies with small residual drift.
  • Plot the mean MSD on linear and log-log scales; choose fit windows deliberately ("fit clip factors"); extract the diffusion coefficient D and the exponent α.
  • Vary the bacterial concentration/activity and repeat: How does D depend on it? Does any drift correlate with concentration? If it does, that is a clean, directional departure from equilibrium.

Refresher — measurement noise on the MSD

A static localization error ε (pixel/subpixel noise) adds a time-independent offset to the MSD: ⟨Δr2meas(t)⟩ = ⟨Δr2(t)⟩ + 2ε2 (in 2D, with cross terms vanishing). On a linear plot this shifts the curve up; on a log-log plot it flattens the short-time end toward a plateau, which can masquerade as a change of regime. Knowing your ε (from immobilized beads) is what lets you trust the short-time slope [15, 16].

6 · Optical tweezers module

A focused laser traps a micron bead in an approximately harmonic potential U = ½kx2; displacement from the trap center therefore reads out force, F = kx [10, 11]. Two independent measurements:

  • Confined Brownian motion (calibration). Hold the bead and record its position distribution around the trap center. Equipartition (via the virial theorem) gives ½k⟨x2⟩ = ½kBT per quadratic degree of freedom — this calibrates the trap stiffness k against the known temperature.
  • Stokes drag (viscosity). Translate the trap at constant velocity v; the bead lags the center by x where kx = 6πηRv. Measuring x(v) — try several velocities — yields the viscosity η.

Together with a diffusion measurement (D = μkBT = kBT/6πηR), the two measurements constitute an explicit test of the Einstein relation in the equilibrium sample — and a baseline against which the active-bath deviations stand out. Use subpixel detection throughout.

Refresher — interface effects (read before comparing samples)

Walls and free surfaces bias Brownian motion. A liquid–gas interface can enhance mobility [12]; a liquid–solid interface (the closed cell used for the tweezers) restricts diffusion and enhances drag [13, 14]. Open-drop and closed-cell measurements are not directly interchangeable — keep the geometry consistent within each comparison.

7 · Preparation questions (from the lab briefing)

Work these before the session; pointers in brackets.

  1. Calculate the diffusion coefficient of spherical beads of radius R = 1 μm in water at 25 °C. [D = kBT/6πηR, ηwater ≈ 0.89 mPa·s → D ≈ 0.25 μm²/s.]
  2. Optical resolution of a 10×/0.25 objective, and of a 63×/1.2? [Abbe: d = λ/(2·NA).]
  3. Position is an intensity-weighted centroid over N pixels, each with independent uncertainty δx — what is the uncertainty of the centroid? [Error propagation, §3 of the briefing; see also 15–17.]
  4. From the Langevin equation, compute ⟨x(t)⟩ and the MSD. [Swap averaging and time integration; the noise correlator is a delta function.]
  5. Add constant drift vdrift: up to what timescale does motion look diffusive? Plot MSD vs time for drift speeds spanning the diffusive, ballistic, and transitional regimes — use log-log. [Crossover when vdrift·t ~ √(2dDt).]
  6. Small static measurement error ε added to positions: how do the linear and log-log MSD plots change? [Plateau 2ε² at short times — see the refresher in §5.]
  7. Use the virial and equipartition theorems to relate T to ⟨U⟩ for U = ½kx²; find k in terms of ⟨x²⟩ and T. [k = kBT/⟨x²⟩.]
  8. A bead of radius R in a trap U = ½kx² under flow v: find the viscosity in terms of the trap displacement x. [η = kx/(6πRv).]

8 · Concept refreshers (stat-mech level, compact)

The fluctuation–dissipation theorem (FDT)

Near equilibrium, the same microscopic collisions that damp motion also drive fluctuations; hence the one-line relation D = μkBT. Kubo's review [1] derives this formally (linear response): the response of a system to a small perturbation is governed by the equilibrium correlation function of the fluctuating quantity. Active media violate the premise — energy is injected mechanically — so the relation fails and "temperature" becomes a fitted diagnostic rather than a state variable.

Equipartition and the virial theorem

In equilibrium each quadratic degree of freedom carries ½kBT of mean energy. For a harmonically trapped bead, ½k⟨x²⟩ = ½kBT per axis — the basis of trap calibration. Wu–Libchaber's b = 1 (rather than b = 3) scaling of vrms2/v02 with bead size is direct evidence that equipartition fails in the bacterial bath [9].

Effective temperature

Forcing D = μkBTeff on non-equilibrium data defines Teff. In the bacterial bath Teff is 10²–10³ × room temperature [9]. Different observables generally yield different Teff — the tell-tale sign that the system has no true temperature.

Microscopy essentials

Numerical aperture NA = n·sinθ sets resolution, d = λ/(2·NA) (Abbe) [5, 6]. Köhler illumination gives even, controlled illumination — run the alignment tutorial before the session [4]. Phase contrast converts phase shifts from transparent specimens (bacteria!) into amplitude contrast [7]. Fluorescence images only the labeled species (the beads) with high contrast [8]. Conjugate planes: knowing which planes are imaged where tells you what each diaphragm does [3].
